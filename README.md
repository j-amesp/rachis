# Rachis
## Disclosure Without Surrender - Integration authority, and federated information exchange as public infrastructure

Organisations that hold information under a duty - security classification, statute, professional confidence or contract - routinely cannot share it with those who need it, and the obstruction is organisational rather than technical. The prevailing commercial response supplies imposed coherence: an external party arrives with a method, compels the organisation to write down what it means, and absorbs the political cost of the decisions. It works, and it produces a dependency that is extremely difficult to unwind.

This document locates that dependency precisely. It is not created by the ontology, the index, the graph or the interface, each of which is well precedented and replaceable. It is created by the integration model - vendor engineers embedded inside the customer, learning the source estate and encoding that knowledge in artefacts the customer cannot read, cannot verify and cannot transfer. Cost is linear in sources and the knowledge accrues privately.
An alternative is specified. The federation publishes an Expectation: a versioned, offline-testable exchange contract adopted unilaterally rather than negotiated. The source owner maps their own schema to it declaratively, declares field by field what may be released, binds the resulting disclosure cryptographically at source, and ships it. The platform verifies and holds no authority to classify and no key to what was withheld. Withheld fields are absent rather than encrypted; pointer fields resolve only by a callback the source may refuse, bound or revoke.

Four decades of prior attempts - MIP, NIEM, NNEC, DCGS and its integration backbone, FMN, X-Road, Gaia-X and International Data Spaces - are examined and reduced to ten transferable lessons that the proposal is then tested against. Seven contributions are claimed: the Expectation as a unilateral offline-testable contract; a JSON and post-quantum profile of the NATO metadata binding standard supporting field-level selective disclosure; identity as a read-time projection over an append-only assertion store rather than a pipeline stage; the comparative failure analysis itself; the connection of classification aggregation to statistical disclosure control, from which secondary suppression and disclosure by differencing are imported as first-class concerns; a formal model of integration authority yielding the result that switching cost is a linear function of privately-held integration knowledge, and therefore that lock-in under supplier-performed integration deepens with adoption independently of supplier conduct; and the application of commons design principles to federated exchange, treating the contract, the registries and the conformance regime as the governed commons rather than the data.
The mechanism that changes the economics is mapping reuse. Mappings describe a source product's schema and contain no data, so they are publishable and reusable across every organisation running that product, and per-participant integration cost falls as the federation grows rather than remaining constant.

The larger claim is one of unbundling. The prevailing model sells the federation layer and the analytical capability as a single purchase, and it is the bundling that produces the dependency: the second cannot be bought without accepting the first. If the federation layer is instead a published contract with a conformance programme and an independent custodian, suppliers compete above the federation rather than being it - the same relocation of competition that occurred when networking moved from proprietary vendor stacks to a published protocol suite.

Aggregation, marking correctness and model governance are not solved, and are stated as such.
